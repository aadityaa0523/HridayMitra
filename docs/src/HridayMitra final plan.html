HridayMitra

A 75-second daily heart check-in station plus a night-time locket. It warns the family, the ASHA worker and the doctor several days before a heart failure patient needs hospital care. It works in any Indian home: rural or urban, with or without internet, with or without reading skills.

Problem statement: Early Warning of Worsening Heart Failure. This is a monitoring idea, not a medical diagnosis tool.

1. Our idea in one paragraph

Every morning, the patient sits for about 75 seconds on a stool placed on a small weighing platform, with bare feet on four steel plates and one finger in a clip. The station measures weight, water in the legs (foot impedance), heart rate, rhythm and blood oxygen. It then asks two questions in the patient's own language, answered with a green or red button. At night, an optional chest locket records breathing rate, whether the patient had to sit up to breathe, and restlessness; during the day it counts steps and has an SOS button. All the thinking happens on the device, which compares today against the patient's own normal. A green, amber or red light and a voice message show the result. A red result goes out by SMS and automatic voice call to the family, the ASHA worker and the doctor, saying why it fired. It needs no smartphone, no internet and no reading, and it runs on batteries through power cuts.

3. Why this approach (what we learned from research)

  • Weight alone is weak. Daily weight rules catch only 23–35% of worsening events, with about 4 false alerts per patient per year (FAST trial, SCALE-HF 1).
  • Weight + foot impedance doubles detection. A scale with foot impedance caught 70% vs 35%, with fewer alerts (Bodyport SCALE-HF 1; one observational, company-linked study).
  • Night breathing and night heart rate are among the best early signs. HeartLogic, the best implant system, relies on them. A locket can pick up night breathing cheaply.
  • Bed sensors fail in Indian homes. Shared beds, charpais, floor mats, blankets and pillows mix up the data, and there's no heart failure evidence for them.
  • Wrist bands get abandoned. About half of users stop wearing them fully, and wrist pulse readings are noisy whenever the hands move.
  • Programs fail when nobody acts, or people stop using them. TIM-HF2 worked because a team responded to every alert. Tele-HF and BEAT-HF fell to ~55% use. So we keep the effort tiny and send alerts to people who act (family + ASHA worker).
  • Almost half of wrong alerts come from bad measurements (TwoCan study). So we check reading quality and ask for a re-check before raising the alarm.

7. How the alert is decided (software)

  1. Quality check: reject a reading if contact is bad, the patient isn't still, the finger signal is noisy, or it's the wrong person (see identity in Section 8). Retry with a voice prompt.
  2. Personal baseline: for each signal, a rolling median and spread (MAD) over days −35 to −7. In the first weeks, start from the hospital "dry weight" and population averages.
  3. Worsening scores: a robust z-score per signal, counting only the worsening direction (weight ↑, impedance ↓, HR ↑, breathing ↑, SpO₂ ↓, activity ↓, upright minutes ↑). Plus published trend detectors: weight moving-average crossover (9 vs 20 days > 0.62 kg) and an impedance cumulative-sum detector (CUSUM) (Gyllensten 2016).
  4. Combined index across three families: fluid (weight, impedance), heart and lungs (HR, breathing, SpO₂, night posture), and symptoms.
  5. Light colour:
    • Amber = index high once → ask for a 30 s re-check.
    • Red = high on 2 of 3 days and at least 2 families involved, or one family high for 3 days.
    • The alert clears only when the index drops well below the trigger level (HeartLogic-style 16 → 6), so it doesn't flicker on and off.
  6. Immediate red flags (no waiting): SpO₂ < 90%, HR > 110 or < 40, new irregular rhythm, "breathless at rest" confirmed twice, or SOS.
  7. Cause tag + explanation: heart-failure-like / infection-like / AF-like / salty-meal-like / after medicine change / dehydration-like. Example: "Weight up 1.8 kg in 6 days. Leg water up. Breathing faster at night. Call ASHA."

10. Hardware and cost

Custom part (for the schematic and simulation marks)

Tetrapolar foot-impedance front end. The classic ESP32's DAC generates a sine wave, which goes through a band-pass filter into an op-amp current source (Howland type, with current limit and DC blocking). The current is injected through the 2 outer foot plates; an instrumentation amplifier senses the voltage on the 2 inner plates; a precision rectifier and low-pass filter feed the ESP32 ADC. A reference resistor is measured at every check-in. The proof is an LTspice simulation showing that sweeping the skin contact resistance (wet vs dry feet) does not change the reading. This replaces an AD5933 module, which costs about ₹3,500.

11. Validation plan (software and honest testing)

  • Synthetic cohort: 200+ virtual patients (the PS asks for at least 20), each with a 30-day warm-up and a 30-day test window. Worsening starts 3–10 days before the hospital visit, with:
    • weight +1–4.5 kg
    • breathing +1.5–2 per minute
    • heart rate +3–8
    • impedance −5 to −15%
    • steps −15 to −30%
    • more upright minutes at night
    30–40% of episodes have no weight gain. Built-in confounders: infection, AF, salty meals, medicine changes, guest users, wet feet, missed days, a forgotten locket.
  • Real data: test the heart-rate and rhythm code on the PhysioNet BIDMC CHF ECG database. Waveforms are simulated with NeuroKit2.
  • Metrics: sensitivity, specificity, median lead time, false alerts per patient per month, plus time in alert and PPV. Split by patient (70/30, tuning only on the training patients), with bootstrap confidence intervals.
  • Targets: beat the weight rule (23–35% sensitivity, ~0.35 false alerts/patient-month). Aim for ≥60% sensitivity at ≤0.21 false alerts/patient-month.
  • Ablations: weight only → + impedance → + vitals → + locket → + symptoms → + re-check → + identity check.
  • Honesty: synthetic results prove the logic finds the patterns we built in, not that it's clinically accurate. We'll say so on the results slide.

14. Risks and honest limits

  • About a third of part prices are estimates, so get quotes first: cost is a pass/fail rule.
  • The DIY impedance circuit is unvalidated against real people; Bodyport's evidence is a single company-linked study.
  • Lung fluid is detected only indirectly (SpO₂, breathing, symptoms).
  • Rhythm checking from a finger clip is weaker than ECG.
  • Pacemaker/ICD safety with injected current is not verified; an impedance-off mode is provided.
  • SIM800L 2G doesn't work on Jio; the 4G module costs more.
  • Results are synthetic; adherence and the response loop in Indian homes are untested.
  • It can't predict sudden cardiac death. It is not an emergency device.

15. After the hackathon (startup path)

  1. Pilot with one district hospital's cardiology team and its ASHA workers, with ethics approval, starting in "shadow mode" (alerts go only to the study team).
  2. Customers: children in cities buying for parents (buy or rent), hospitals (90-day post-discharge kits), and government NCD programmes.
  3. CDSCO registration (likely Class B/C), IEC 60601 safety testing, and a custom PCB to bring cost down.
